This time it is not a game. Nothing to win, nothing to lose: you watch, you
listen, and you let yourself be told a fable every French child learns at
school. The story is told in French, with La Fontaine's own words on screen.

It is an experiment, and it pulled me out of my habits in two ways.

**A change of pace.** Until now I made little games here where you tap, jump
and dodge. This was the opposite idea: slow down. A camera gliding gently over
the savannah, long enough to see the lion breathe in his sleep and the rat
climb out of the ground, a little dazed.

**A voice.** The first time I used an AI-generated voice. I wanted a male
storyteller, deep and warm, like the ones on the fairy-tale records of my
childhood. I found him on ElevenLabs, on the free plan: about 800 credits for
ten short clips.

## The prompt

It all started with a slightly hesitant sentence one evening (typed in French,
translated here):

> "Could we make a nice animation based on Jean de La Fontaine's fable, the
> Lion and the Rat? On the web, but with care for detail and slow scrolling so
> you can really see the animals. Well, it's an idea. A plan?"

Claude suggested splitting it into scenes, and three art directions:
watercolour, paper cut-outs or engraving. I picked engraving, like an old book:
sepia ink, hatching, laid paper.

## The iterations

About ten rounds, and every one of them was about what I *saw*, never about
the code (all typed in French, translated here):

1. **"On my phone it stops moving after 1 min 19."** The forest scene, too
   heavy to draw on a phone. Fixed.
2. **"Can the lion's head be prettier? The rat's too? The net doesn't cover
   the whole lion."** The lion had a dog's head and a porcupine's mane. He now
   has a real mane falling in locks.
3. **"When the lion walks, he doesn't touch the ground."** He now puts each paw
   down, one after the other.
4. **"The night isn't dark enough, you can see the trees appear."** The night
   became a true black, with a moon and stars.
5. **"The page gives too much room to the technology."** Everything went,
   except a thin progress bar in a black frame.

Then the voice, clip by clip: ten blocks of text to paste into ElevenLabs, with
pauses and a spelling trick so the narrator would say La Fontaine's *rets*
(nets) the right way. One line, a question, came out flat; a second take with
a more expressive setting fixed it. The long sentences were cut so the words
land on the picture: the net falls just as you hear it.

## What I learned

The crickets, the wind, the birds and the rat's squeaks and nibbling are all
made inside the page, with no sound file. The roar would not be faked: it is a
real one, given away under a free licence. And the moral has not aged a day:
*patience and time do more than strength or rage.*
